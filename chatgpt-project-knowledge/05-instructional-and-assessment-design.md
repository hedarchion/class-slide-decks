# Instructional and assessment design reference

## Lesson mapping

1. State one observable objective.
2. Identify prerequisite knowledge and at least one likely misconception.
3. Retrieve 2–4 essential pieces of prior learning, including explanation or application.
4. Make the bridge to the new objective explicit.
5. Model the target, guide practice, check understanding, explain corrections, and require transfer.
6. Allocate one idea or decision per slide; split dense content instead of shrinking it.

For each slide ask: Does it elicit the intended thinking? Is the action obvious within seconds? Is every visual/text element necessary? Is new content separated from demanding practice? Does feedback explain why an answer works and why a plausible alternative fails?

## Cognitive load and accessibility

Signal the important part. Place labels next to what they describe. Do not project paragraphs that the teacher reads verbatim. Reveal procedures successively. Delay answers until students have thinking time. Do not depend on color alone, muted text, thin strokes, drag-and-drop, or content below the fold.

## Formative interactions

Choose the smallest interaction that reveals useful thinking.

| Pattern | Evidence | Feedback requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multiple choice | misconception or strategy | explain the correct choice and contrast a distractor |
| Ordering | procedural understanding | show the correct order and dependency |
| Categorising | concept boundaries | explain each defining feature |
| Error analysis | diagnostic reasoning | identify the exact error and repair it |
| Confidence check | readiness | route toward support or extension |
| Predict/reveal | prior model or inference | require commitment before revealing |

Every interaction needs a clear prompt, response path, teacher-controlled reveal/check, explanatory feedback, reset, keyboard access, and a non-digital fallback.

## Assessment blueprint

Map each observable outcome to multiple pieces of evidence when practical. Mix recognition, controlled construction, explanation, and fresh application. Keep the learner version separate from the key or rubric. State the intended use and avoid overclaiming what one unpiloted task proves.

For extended writing, build a content spine through earlier tasks. A useful progression is recognition → sentence construction → planning prompts → independent text. Rubrics should distinguish task fulfilment, language control, organisation/cohesion, and relevant vocabulary where those dimensions match the objective.

## Common lesson-design responses to learner needs

- Present used for finished past time: return to the time anchor.
- Past form after `did/didn't`: show that the auxiliary carries past marking and the lexical verb returns to base form.
- Missing third-person singular `-s`: contrast `I play` with `she plays` orally.
- `-s` after `does/doesn't`: point to the base verb after the auxiliary.
- Wrong `am/is/are`: map subject to the correct form before adding `verb-ing`.
- Present simple used for an action in progress: contrast routine signals with “now/look/listen” context.
- Understandable but unnatural collocation: test habitual word partnership, not loose semantic plausibility.
- Conjunction chosen by sound: name the relationship between clauses before selecting a linker.
- Reading-detail trap: match every requirement and cite the exact textual evidence.
- Weak complaint language: build complaint → reason → response → solution, maintaining politeness.
